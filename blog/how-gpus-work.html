How GPUs work, with the B200

2026.08.20 · Hyeondo Jang

A GPU has two budgets — how fast it can compute, and how fast it can be fed. This post is about the second one. We walk down the B200 from the die to the SM to the memory hierarchy, and end at the one ratio that decides how fast an LLM can decode.

3The memory hierarchy

Four numbers describe the whole hierarchy, and they are the only four worth memorising. Registers and L1 are per SM, so their chip-wide totals come from multiplying by 160:

Level Where it sits Per SM Whole chip Bandwidth
Registers inside each subpartition 256 KB 41 MB 100+ TB/s
L1 + shared one block per SM 256 KB 41 MB tens of TB/s
L2 on the compute die, shared — 126 MB ≈ 12 TB/s
HBM3E stacked beside the die — 288 GB 8 TB/s

Read the last two columns together and the figure's shape falls out. Capacity climbs by a factor of 2000 in a single step at the bottom — 126 MB of L2, then 288 GB of HBM — while bandwidth falls the whole way down. There is no level that is both fast and large, and the jump happens in the wrong place: exactly where a model has to live.

How badly it lands is worth stating in units of a real model. Qwen3-8B in BF16 is 16.4 GB, which is about 80× all on-chip SRAM combined. L2 holds 0.77% of it. And it is not as though a well-written kernel could stage the model layer by layer: one Qwen3-8B decoder block is 193M parameters, or 386 MB in BF16 — already three times what L2 can hold. Not one layer fits, let alone 36.

So there is no choice to make about where the weights live. They live in HBM, and every forward pass has to pull them up through the bottom arrow of the figure.

The one asymmetry

Per generation, compute grows roughly 4× (2× from the architecture, 2× from a narrower number format), bandwidth roughly 2×, and capacity by less than 1.4×. The gap the figure shows is not a flaw in this particular chip — it widens with every generation.
