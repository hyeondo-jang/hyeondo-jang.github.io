How GPUs work, with the B200

2026.08.20 · Hyeondo Jang

A GPU has two budgets — how fast it can compute, and how fast it can be fed. This post is about the second one. We walk down the B200 from the die to the SM to the memory hierarchy, and end at the one ratio that decides how fast an LLM can decode.

3The memory hierarchy

Four numbers describe the whole hierarchy, and they are the only four worth memorising. Registers and L1 are per SM, so their chip-wide totals come from multiplying by 160:

Level Where it sits Per SM Whole chip Bandwidth
Registers inside each subpartition 256 KB 41 MB 100+ TB/s
L1 + shared one block per SM 256 KB 41 MB tens of TB/s
L2 on the compute die, shared — 126 MB ≈ 12 TB/s
HBM3E stacked beside the die — 192 GB 8 TB/s

Read the last two columns together and the figure's shape falls out. Capacity climbs by a factor of 2000 in a single step at the bottom — 126 MB of L2, then 288 GB of HBM — while bandwidth falls the whole way down. There is no level that is both fast and large, and the jump happens in the wrong place: exactly where a model has to live.

How badly it lands is worth stating in units of a real model. Qwen3-8B quantized to FP8 is 8.2 GB — one byte per parameter — which is about 40× all on-chip SRAM combined. L2 holds 1.5% of it. And it is not as though a well-written kernel could stage the model layer by layer: one Qwen3-8B decoder block is 193M parameters, or 193 MB in FP8 — still more than L2 can hold. Not one layer fits, let alone 36.

So there is no choice to make about where the weights live. They live in HBM, and every forward pass has to pull them up through the bottom arrow of the figure.

The shape of the problem

The hierarchy is the wrong shape for a language model. Every level fast enough to keep the tensor cores busy holds under 1.5% of Qwen3-8B; the only level that holds the whole model is the slowest one — and it is the level every forward pass has to reach.

6The ratio: 562 FLOP per byte

Everything in the figure above collapses into a single number. A processor has two independent budgets — a peak rate $\peak$ in FLOP/s and a bandwidth $\bw$ in byte/s — and a kernel is characterised by its arithmetic intensity, the FLOPs it performs per byte it pulls from memory:

\begin{equation} \AI \;\defeq\; \frac{\text{FLOPs performed}}{\text{bytes moved from memory}} \qquad [\FLOP/\Bytes] \end{equation}

The kernel can be limited by either budget, never beaten by both, so the best it can attain is whichever runs out first:

\begin{equation} \text{attainable FLOP/s} \;=\; \min\big(\peak,\; \bw \cdot \AI\big) \label{eq:roofline} \end{equation}

The crossover is the ridge point $\ridge = \peak/\bw$. For a B200 in FP8:

\begin{equation} \ridge \;=\; \frac{4500\ \mathrm{TFLOP/s}}{8\ \mathrm{TB/s}} \;=\; \mathbf{562\ \FLOP/\Bytes} \end{equation}

Said in words: a B200 can perform 562 FP8 operations in the time it takes to fetch one byte. Hand it a kernel with less intensity than that and the tensor cores wait, no matter how well the kernel is written.

Why only the bottom arrow enters the calculation

It is fair to ask why $\bw$ is the HBM number when a byte actually crosses three links to reach a tensor core. Because the links are in series, and a series of pipes runs at the width of its narrowest section. Line the four up:

8  <  ≈12  <  tens  <  100+   (TB/s)

HBM is the smallest, so HBM sets the rate and the upper three drop out of the arithmetic entirely. This is not a coincidence of one chip: the whole point of a cache hierarchy is that each level up is faster, which guarantees the bottom link is the slow one. What is unusual about a GPU is how little headroom the next level up has — L2 is only about 1.5× HBM, where a CPU's L2 is an order of magnitude faster than its DRAM.

The one thing that could invalidate the substitution is reuse: if a byte were fetched from HBM once and then read many times out of L2, the HBM traffic would be smaller than the bytes the kernel consumes. During decode that does not happen — L2's hit rate is essentially zero (§5) — so every byte the kernel touches is a byte HBM had to deliver, and $\bw = 8$ TB/s is exactly right.

Where decode lands

Now put a real workload on the axis. Generating one token from Qwen3-8B in FP8 reads all 8.2 GB of weights and does two FLOPs per parameter — one multiply, one add. One byte per parameter, two FLOPs per parameter:

\begin{equation} \AI \;=\; \frac{2N}{1\,\Bytes \times N} \;=\; 2\ \FLOP/\Bytes \end{equation}

Two, against a ridge point of 562. Batch-1 decode sits more than 280× to the left of where the chip stops being bandwidth-bound, which by \eqref{eq:roofline} means it can attain $8 \times 2 = 16$ TFLOP/s — 0.36% of the B200's peak. The other 99.64% of the silicon is idle, and no amount of kernel engineering changes that, because the limit is not in the kernel.

The same fact stated as a clock, which is the form that matters when you are waiting for tokens:

The batch-1 decode ceiling on one B200
Per generated tokenBytesTimeTokens/s
Weights only (short context)8.2 GB1.03 ms975
Weights + 40k of KV cache11.2 GB1.39 ms717

Divide bytes by 8 TB/s. That is the entire calculation — no FLOP count appears in it, because none of it is compute.

Why a newer chip is not the answer

562 looks like it should be getting worse every generation, and the usual story says it is: compute grows faster than bandwidth, so the ridge point drifts right and each new chip demands more arithmetic per byte. Check it against this pair, though, and the story does not hold — at a fixed number format the ridge barely moved, and it moved the helpful way:

Ridge pointH100B200
BF16989 / 3.35 = 2952250 / 8 = 281
FP81979 / 3.35 = 5914500 / 8 = 562

Blackwell improved bandwidth 2.4× and same-format compute 2.3×, so within a format the ratio is flat. The reason 562 is twice 281 is not the generation at all — it is the column, not the row. The ceiling doubles when the number format halves.

Which is the interesting part, because halving the format halves the bytes too. Every other lever on a hardware roadmap raises a ceiling that batch-1 decode was never within two orders of magnitude of. A narrower format is the one change that moves the side of the ratio decode actually lives on. That is why quantization is not a compression trick in this setting — it is the only hardware-side move that touches the binding constraint, and it is why the software-side moves that remain all have the same shape: make fewer bytes cross the bottom arrow per generated token.

The sentence this whole post exists to set up

A B200 wants 562 FLOP per byte. Generating one token from an 8 GB model offers it 2. Everything that follows — batching, paged attention, MoE, quantization, MLA, sparse attention, speculative decoding — is a different way of raising that number.

What those bytes are made of, and why 8.2 GB of weights turns into 11.2 GB once the context is long, is the subject of the companion post: How LLMs work, with Qwen3-8B.
